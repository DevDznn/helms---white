The Lismore Emergency Dental Guide: What to Do in the First 60 Minutes of a Dental Crisis

When a tooth is knocked out, pain becomes severe or a restoration breaks, a calm first response can protect the area while you arrange professional care.

In this article

Start with safety Knocked-out tooth Pain or swelling Broken tooth Lost restoration Calling reception

A dental emergency can be stressful, but the first priority is straightforward: protect the person, protect the tooth where possible and contact a dentist promptly. The advice below is general information for common situations and does not replace an individual assessment.

Start with safety

Check for significant facial or head injury before focusing on the tooth. Call Triple Zero or seek hospital emergency care for difficulty breathing or swallowing, uncontrolled bleeding, severe facial swelling, loss of consciousness or major trauma.

Dental emergency now?

Call the practice as soon as possible.

Describe what happened, when it happened, where the pain or damage is located and whether there is swelling or bleeding.

Scenario 01

A permanent tooth has been knocked out

A knocked-out permanent tooth is time-sensitive. Handle it as little as possible and act promptly.

  1. 01

    Hold the crown

    Pick the tooth up by the white chewing surface. Avoid touching or scraping the root.

  2. 02

    Clean only when necessary

    When the tooth is dirty, rinse it briefly and gently with milk or sterile saline. Do not scrub it or use soap.

  3. 03

    Keep it moist

    A cooperative adult may gently place the tooth back in its socket in the correct direction. Otherwise, store it in milk or saline and take it straight to the dentist.

  4. 04

    Call immediately

    Tell reception that a permanent tooth has been completely knocked out and approximately how long ago it occurred.

Important: Do not attempt to reinsert a knocked-out baby tooth.

Scenario 02

Severe toothache, an abscess or facial swelling

Intense throbbing pain, tenderness when biting, a bad taste, fever or swelling may indicate infection. A dental abscess does not resolve on its own and needs prompt professional care.

  • Rinse gently with warm salty water.
  • Use over-the-counter pain relief only as directed on the packaging and when it is safe for you.
  • Apply a wrapped cold pack to the outside of the face for short intervals when swelling follows injury.
  • Do not place aspirin, crushed tablets or chemicals directly on the tooth or gum.
  • Contact the dentist promptly rather than waiting for the pain to settle.

Seek emergency medical care

Breathing or swallowing difficulty requires urgent help.

Call Triple Zero when swelling affects breathing, swallowing or the airway, or when the person appears seriously unwell.

Scenario 03

A tooth is chipped, cracked or fractured

The severity of a broken tooth is not always obvious. Even when discomfort is mild, the tooth should be assessed so the dentist can check for deeper cracks, nerve exposure or injury to the surrounding tissues.

  • Collect any clean tooth fragments and bring them with you.
  • Rinse the mouth gently with clean water.
  • Avoid biting on the affected tooth and avoid very hot, cold or sweet foods when it is sensitive.
  • Cover a sharp edge temporarily with dental wax when it is irritating the cheek or tongue.

Scenario 04

A filling, crown or bridge has come out

A lost restoration can leave the underlying tooth sensitive and less protected. Keep the restoration if you can find it and arrange an appointment.

  • Keep the crown or bridge clean and bring it to the visit.
  • Avoid chewing on the affected side.
  • Pharmacy temporary dental material may provide short-term coverage when used exactly as directed.
  • Never use household glue or another non-dental adhesive in the mouth.

What to tell reception

Clear information helps the team assess urgency and prepare for your arrival.

What happened?
Trauma, sudden pain, swelling or a lost restoration
When did it begin?
The approximate time of injury or onset of symptoms
What symptoms are present?
Pain, bleeding, swelling, fever or sensitivity
What have you done so far?
Any pain relief, rinsing, cold pack or tooth storage

This article provides general information only. A dentist or medical professional needs to assess your individual situation.
