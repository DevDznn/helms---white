The Dental Decoder: How Evidence-Based Care Supports Better Decisions

Evidence-based dentistry is not a single test or technology. It is a way of combining reliable research, professional judgement and the needs and preferences of the person receiving care.

In this article

What it means Looking for the cause Conservative care Explaining alternatives Standards and systems Questions to ask

Good dental decisions are rarely based on one factor alone. The condition of the tooth, the strength of the available evidence, the dentist’s clinical experience, expected longevity, cost and your own priorities all matter.

What evidence-based dentistry means

Evidence-based care brings together three important elements: current research, the clinician’s skills and experience, and the patient’s values, circumstances and preferences. The aim is not simply to follow a standard formula. It is to apply good evidence thoughtfully to the person in front of the dentist.

01

Research

Reliable clinical evidence helps compare likely benefits, limitations and risks.

02

Clinical judgement

Examination findings and professional experience shape how that evidence applies.

03

Your circumstances

Health, priorities, preferences and practical considerations remain part of the decision.

Principle 01

Look for the cause, not only the immediate problem

Repairing damage is important, but long-term planning also asks why the problem developed. Tooth decay, for example, may be influenced by cleaning habits, diet, dry mouth, medication, previous restorations or the way teeth contact each other.

Understanding contributing factors can help reduce the chance of the same pattern continuing after treatment.

Principle 02

Preserve healthy tooth structure where appropriate

Conservative care means avoiding unnecessary treatment while still addressing disease and structural risk. Sometimes monitoring, prevention or a smaller restoration is suitable. At other times, a stronger restoration is recommended because too little healthy tooth remains for a filling to last predictably.

The useful question

Why is this option appropriate for this tooth?

A clear explanation should connect the recommendation to the examination findings, remaining tooth structure and expected outcome.

Principle 03

Explain the reasonable alternatives

Informed decisions require more than the name of a recommended procedure. Your dentist should explain what the treatment is intended to achieve, what alternatives are reasonable, what may happen without treatment and what limitations apply.

Purpose
What problem the treatment is intended to address
Alternatives
Other reasonable approaches, including monitoring where suitable
Trade-offs
Differences in strength, appearance, time, cost and longevity
Next steps
What is urgent and what can be planned over time

Principle 04

Support decisions with consistent clinical systems

Evidence-based care also depends on reliable practice systems. Clear records, infection-prevention procedures, appropriate imaging, maintenance protocols and regular review help make treatment safer and more consistent.

Accreditation and clinical governance do not replace individual judgement, but they help create a structured environment in which recommendations can be documented, reviewed and explained.

Principle 05

Value continuity and review

Oral health changes over time. Seeing a team that understands your history can make it easier to compare new findings with previous records, review how earlier treatment has performed and adjust preventive care when circumstances change.

Questions you can ask before treatment

  • What did you find during the examination?
  • What is the treatment intended to achieve?
  • Are there reasonable alternatives?
  • What are the likely benefits, limitations and risks?
  • How urgent is the decision?
  • What costs and treatment stages should I expect?

General information cannot determine which treatment is suitable for an individual tooth. A clinical examination is required.
